Why Dayak beadwork

What it brings to shelves around the world

A living Borneo tradition

The aso’, the hornbill, the standing ancestor figure: motifs Dayak beaders have carried for generations on baby carriers, sun hats and ceremonial dress. Every piece in this line is new work in that language: contemporary product, traditional grammar.

Handwork you can count

A 120 cm table runner carries roughly 28,000 glass beads, threaded one by one onto cotton. One beader, about three weeks. Nothing is printed, nothing is moulded. The pattern is built bead by bead, and it shows at arm’s length.

Not already everywhere

Buyers around the world know Indian and Central American beadwork on sight. Dayak work reads differently, with its dense dark grounds, mirrored hooks and guardian figures, and it sits naturally among linen, rattan and stoneware. A story the shop two streets over is not telling.

Motifs & Meaning

How to read the beadwork

Dayak beadwork carries an old cosmology in colour, figure and form. This page reads it the way the village does — and it is written to be borrowed, freely, for your shelf cards and product pages.

The frame

Two worlds, held in balance

Dayak art begins with a divided universe. The upper world belongs to the sky and its great messenger, the hornbill; the lower world is water, home of the serpent and the aso’. Neither is evil and neither rules alone — the point of the universe, and of the art, is the balance between them.

Joining the two stands a tree. The Ngaju call it batang garing, the Kayan kayo urip — the tree of life, roots in the underworld, crown in the sky, the human world resting on its trunk. People live mid-tree, between powers, and the work of a good life is keeping the two ends in conversation.

Hold that picture and the beadwork starts to explain itself. Compositions pair and mirror; a bird answers a serpent; borders run in matched halves. The symmetry is not decoration. It is the balance of the worlds, restated in glass.

The figures

Four families of pattern populate that universe. Most beadwork carries at least one of them.

Shot · 1:1 · Aso’ motif in beads, close enough to count.

Motif family 01

Aso’

the dragon-dog

Part dog, part serpent, the aso’ is the great guardian figure of Kayan and Kenyah art — a creature of the watery lower world, feminine in nature, guarding fertility and the family. On beadwork its body unwinds into hooks and spirals until it fills the field edge to edge. You read the curl of a jaw here, a tail there, inside what first looks like pure geometry. Traditionally it protected what mattered most: baby carriers, shields, the houses of leading families.

Motif family 02

Tinggang

the hornbill

In Dayak cosmology the rhinoceros hornbill is the bird of the upper world, flying down into this one as a messenger of the gods and an omen bird — and today it is the emblem of Kalimantan itself. Beaders render the sweep of its casque and tail as long curved lines that give a composition its movement. On a contemporary shelf it reads simply as a strong, strange, beautiful bird.

A yellow hornbill holds the centre of the black field, its long body echoed by green curls.

Mirrored red curls form a central figure, held between white, turquoise and yellow companions.

Motif family 03

Kelunan

the human figure

A standing figure, arms raised, faceted into the bead grid. In older work the right to wear the figure was graded by rank: the full figure for the highest families (the deta’u), the head alone for lesser aristocrats, abstract curves for everyone else — and wearing above one’s station was believed to invite illness. On a baby carrier the figure stood as ancestor and guardian over the child who rode beneath it. The figures beaded in Kampung Manik today are new drawings in that tradition, made for anyone.

Motif family 04

Mata punai & the borders

the dove’s eye, zigzags, paired hooks

The small repeating patterns (mata punai, the dove’s-eye diamond, with its zigzag and hook companions) are the grammar that frames every larger motif. They are also where the beaders improvise: a colour swapped, a border traded with a neighbour, a signature in the margins.

Red, white, turquoise and yellow rings turn repeated curls and small stars into complete borders.

The palette

Colour as prayer

The first thing anyone notices about Dayak beadwork is that the colours collide. That collision is the point. Strong colour set against strong colour is read as harmony — a life in balance with nature and the Creator — and a beader choosing her palette is composing something close to a prayer.

Merah

Red

The spirit of life.

Kuning

Yellow

Majesty and the sacred.

Hijau

Green

The essence of the natural world.

Putih

White

Purity of faith in the Creator.

Hitam

Black

Wards off harm.

Biru

Blue

Strength that does not fade.

In classic Kenyah work the field itself is usually black, yellow and white; red, green and blue arrive as accents. Look at any beaded panel with that key and the composition begins to sort itself.

The shapes

Form, movement, sound

The triangle

A triangle read upward is pucuk rebung, the bamboo shoot — a form known across Kalimantan and the wider archipelago as a sign of growth, new life and hope. Bamboo does not grow back where it was cut; it sends up a new shoot beside the old. The triangle carries that promise.

Mirror symmetry

Compositions pair left and right, and the pairing is cosmology as much as aesthetics: upper world and lower world, each acknowledged, neither dominant. When a panel mirrors itself down the centre line, it is keeping the universe’s accounts in order.

Fringe and dangles

On the old Kenyah baby carriers, ornament worked twice over. Beauty was functional: a bright beaded panel attracted the child’s wandering soul and persuaded it to stay. The hanging strands did the opposite work — moving, clacking, catching light — to startle away whatever should not come near. Fringe is protection you can hear.

The material

The beads themselves

The beads themselves are not local material, and that is part of the meaning. Glass beads reached Borneo along the old trade routes — from India, Venice, China — and the interior adopted them so completely that they became pusaka: heirlooms, handed down, named, remembered.

The most prized old beads carried astonishing value; a single lukut sekala, the Kayan’s most treasured bead, could be exchanged for goods worth a small fortune. The beads sewn in Kampung Manik today are new glass — but they are strung into that long history, one row at a time.

A note on cultural respect

These motifs belong to living Dayak traditions. Everything we sell is new work, designed for contemporary homes and made in Kampung Manik by women of the Dayak, Banjar and Bugis communities, paid at rates agreed with their beading groups. We do not sell ceremonial objects or antiques, and nothing on this site should be read as one. Meanings also vary — between Dayak peoples, between villages, between sources; what is written here is the reading most common in East Kalimantan, told plainly.

Our makers

Kampung Manik, the Bead Village

One neighbourhood in Samarinda, on the Mahakam River, where beadwork is the daily craft of its women. This is where every piece we sell is made. Watch first; the film says it better than the page does.

The women of Kampung Manik at their work: threading, counting, talking. Filmed in the village, Samarinda, East Kalimantan. Privacy-enhanced embed: nothing loads from YouTube until you press play.

Samarinda sits on the Mahakam, the river that was East Kalimantan’s road before there were roads. In one of its kampungs, Kampung Manik, literally the Bead Village, beadwork is not a workshop trade but a neighbourhood one. It happens on porches and living-room floors, in the hours between school runs and cooking, by women who learned it the way you learn a language spoken at home.

The tradition is Dayak: the motifs, the colour logic, the dense grounds all come from the beadwork of Borneo’s indigenous peoples. The village, like most of Samarinda, is more than one community. Dayak, Banjar and Bugis women live on the same lanes and bead the same patterns. Neighbours taught neighbours, mothers teach daughters, and the craft crossed community lines a generation ago without ever leaving home. Three heritages keep one art form working.

The work itself is slower than photographs suggest. Beads go on one at a time: a table runner is roughly 28,000 of them, about three weeks of one woman’s hands. Patterns are counted, not printed; a mistake is unpicked bead by bead. Pay is per piece, at rates agreed with each beading group, and a wholesale order arrives here as weeks of that work, planned around the household, not against it.

Alana Wina Trudi is a founder-led company based in Samarinda. Orders are coordinated directly with the beading groups, with no agents between the village and your purchase order, and every piece is checked in Samarinda before it ships.

Materials & sourcing

Three materials do almost all of the work: glass seed beads, bought by the kilo in Samarinda’s craft market; cotton thread and canvas; and natural rattan for frames, trays and bag bodies. Brass fittings appear where a piece needs them. Material declarations ship with every order; care and handling notes are included in the carton.
